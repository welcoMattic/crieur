How tide pools survive the summer heat

By Daniel Hart on July 3, 2026

Every summer afternoon, the animals of the rocky shore face a test. The tide goes out, the sun heats the rocks, and the small pools of water that remain become hot, salty, and low in oxygen. Most of these animals survive anyway.

At low tide on the Oregon coast, Dr. Maya Okafor kneels beside a pool the size of a kitchen sink. She points to a cluster of purple sea urchins and a few green anemones. "Two hours ago, this water was the same temperature as the ocean," she says. "Now it is almost ten degrees warmer, and it will get warmer still before the tide comes back."

Okafor studies how intertidal animals respond to heat stress. Her team has placed small temperature sensors in 40 pools along a two-mile stretch of coast. The sensors record the water temperature every five minutes, all year long.

Hot water holds less oxygen

Heat is only part of the problem. Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen than cold water, and the animals in the pool continue to breathe. In a crowded pool on a sunny day, oxygen levels can fall by more than half in a single afternoon. At the same time, evaporation makes the water saltier.

Animals have several ways to cope. Many snails close the opening of their shells and slow their metabolism until the tide returns. Anemones pull in their tentacles to reduce the surface that is exposed to the warm water. Some crabs simply leave the pool and hide in cool, damp cracks in the rock.

Proteins that protect the cells

Inside the cells, a second defense begins. When the temperature rises, many animals produce heat shock proteins. These proteins help other proteins keep their correct shape, so that the cells continue to work. The response has a cost, because it uses energy that the animal could otherwise use to grow or reproduce.

"An animal that survives a hot afternoon is not free," Okafor explains. "It pays for that survival. If the hot days come one after another, the animal has no time to recover." Her data show that the number of days above a critical temperature has increased over the last decade.

What the long-term records show

Long-term records help researchers see these changes. A volunteer group has counted the animals in the same pools every spring since 1998. Their counts show that some species have moved to lower positions on the shore, where the pools stay cooler for longer. Other species have become less common.

For Okafor, the volunteers are essential. "We can measure temperature with sensors, but we need people to count the animals," she says. As the tide starts to come back in, she packs her notebook and walks up the beach. Behind her, the first cold waves reach the pool, and the anemones slowly open again.
